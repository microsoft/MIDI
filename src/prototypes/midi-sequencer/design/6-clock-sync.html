  1. Its own tempo, or someone else's. By default the Tempo and meter track drives everything. Switch to another device's clock and the sequencer starts, stops and moves when that device says so.
  2. A good pairing with MIDI Clock. Here the master is the Windows MIDI Clock app, sending into a loopback. The same clock can drive the sequencer and any hardware it also feeds.
  3. Honest about following. A followed clock is looser than the internal one, and the dialog says why and what to do instead.
  4. Send clock to several devices. Each can get clock, start and stop, and song position, with its own offset to line up a slow device. This is the same idea as the offsets in MIDI Clock.
  5. MIDI 2.0 tempo. Devices that speak MIDI 2.0 can also get Set Tempo messages, which tell them the tempo directly instead of making them work it out from clock pulses.
  6. MIDI Time Code out. For video and lighting gear. Following incoming time code is not planned: MIDI Clock made the same call for the same reasons.
