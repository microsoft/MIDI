Each of the three fails somewhere different. Cathode is the only one whose dimmest slot can carry type, because it has no dim end — it is all light and no color. Green is the only one where every slot survives inverse video. Amber has the widest pair. Nobody wins outright, and the theme picker should say what each one is for rather than ranking them.

Why green was the readable one. Not nostalgia. Green carries 71.5 % of luminance in sRGB against red's 21 and blue's 7, so the same beam energy buys far more apparent brightness. The standard slot here sits at 55.8 % luminance on a glass that is darker than either of the others.

And it looks louder than it measures. The three retro comps draw the same controls in the same places, so a pixel sweep of the deck compares the themes and not the content. Mean luminance comes out at 6.2 % for Cathode, 4.6 % for Amber and 4.7 % here — Cathode is the brightest of the three. Green still reads as the loudest, because chroma is not luminance and no contrast check counts it. Worth knowing before somebody turns a theme down to fix a problem that was never brightness.

All of it free — and that is the point

Bone asked for a light source. Cathode asked for scan lines and a resting glow. Amber asked for a bloom color that is not a shade of the hue. Terminal Green asks for nothing. It is those properties at different values, and even the faceplate reflection is one more stop in the deck overlay that already carries the scan lines and the corner falloff. Three retro themes, and the third one costing zero is the strongest evidence yet that the theme model is finished.

The thing memory gets wrong

The glass is not green. Every photograph of a real terminal shows a cool blue-slate faceplate, because it is tinted and anti-glare, and the phosphor sits a long way from it in hue. Built from memory it would have come out as green text on a dark green screen, which is Cathode with the colors swapped and would have made two of the three look like the same idea. And the room is in the glass — a soft highlight across the upper left is in every photograph, and it is what makes a faceplate read as glass rather than as paint.

Delta E is the one that answers "can two controls be told apart", because contrast only counts brightness and scores a change of hue at nothing.

Which of the three to reach for. Terminal Green is the everyday one: the highest rim contrast of the set, the only one where every slot still works filled and labelled, and a hue far enough from its own glass that a busy page stays legible. Amber Console is warmer and has the widest pair, so it suits a page that leans hard on grouping by color. Cathode is the quiet one and the strict one — no color at all, so it is only right for a page laid out to be read by position. All three ask for ordinary color vision and all three degrade to the same thing without it: a rising brightness ladder and nothing else. That is worth the theme picker saying out loud, and High contrast stays one press away. A last shared rule. None of the three sets a typeface. Monospace is what everybody pictures for all three and all three starter layouts use Cascadia Mono, but the font belongs to the layout — a theme that reflowed somebody's labels because they liked the color would be doing something it was never asked to do.
