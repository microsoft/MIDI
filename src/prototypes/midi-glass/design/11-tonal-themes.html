The light one is the real test. The value bar had to stop glowing and start being a dark, saturated ink instead.

Free — what these reuse

The six hue slots, the deck, corner rounding, glass tint set to fully opaque and glow strength set to zero. All four are already theme properties in the editor on screen 8. A customer can slide glow back up on Pigment Dark and land halfway between it and Studio Dark, because they are the same system with different numbers.

Two additions — what they need

Fill at rest, outlined or tonal — one property, and it is what makes these read as a different family. And a track color for the empty part of a fader slot and a knob arc, which the dark themes get away with hardcoding as black. That second one is really a gap in the theme model I should have caught, not a cost of these themes.

What I did not take

Drop shadows. The older Material look puts a real shadow under every card, which would be eighty composition shadows on a surface that has to stay smooth under a finger. The newer approach carries elevation as a tint of the surface's own color instead, and a tint is free. If you want the shadowed look later, that is the conversation to have — it is the one thing here with a real cost.

One thing to decide. These break the "nothing is saturated at rest" rule that keeps a busy Studio Dark page readable — a tonal page is colorful when nothing is happening, so activity has less room to stand out. On a forty-control mixer that is a fair trade for how much friendlier it looks; on a hundred-and-twenty-control page I think Studio Dark still wins. Worth naming that in the theme picker rather than letting someone find out during a set. Names are placeholders — Studio Paper and Studio Slate would sit closer to the existing family if you would rather they read as siblings of Studio Dark.
