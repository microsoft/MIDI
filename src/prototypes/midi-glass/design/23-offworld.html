Screen 23 — Off-world Colonies, after the look of the film Blade Runner: a city where it never stops raining. A wall of cast concrete blocks, stained by years of water and still wet, with dark worn equipment bolted to it and neon for light. The first theme where the wall is the point. Every theme so far has a deck that is a color, a gradient or a grain. This one is a pattern, repeated at its own size, with rain over it. And its lights flare.

WHY THE WALL STAYS BEHIND
Read off the render: on bare wall, 95 pixels in 100 are within 1.36 : 1 of its middle, and the brightest, where a rain streak crosses a lit edge in the light from the blinds, is 1.9 : 1. A module is 1.09 : 1. So the wall can be seen but never competes with a control, which says its state at 3 : 1 or better. The dim print is 74 per cent of the print, not the usual 60. At 60 it failed three ways: on the brightest rain streak (2.84 : 1), inside a lit pad's glow running down the wall (3.67), and under the tail of a flare (3.96). At 74 the worst of those is 4.70, and the rain is drawn at the strength that allows it.

Built — what the engine already does for this wall

A plate of dark metal (PlateColor, PlateEndColor) with a light line along its top (PlateHighlightPercent) and a shadow under it (PlateElevation). A button that is on takes 16 per cent of its color (FillWhenOnPercent) and lights the strip along its foot (ValueStrip). A knob is a gunmetal face (KnobFaceColor) with its line on it (PointerOnCap, PointerColor), in a ring of its own color (ArcTrackHuePercent 16) that glows (ArcGlow). A fader's cap is gunmetal (ThumbColor). Pads are their own family (PadFillAtRest, PadFillWhenOnPercent). Sections are modules (PanelFill, PanelColor, PanelElevation) named in their color (SectionNameInHue). The corners fall away (VignettePercent), and the meter runs cyan, amber, red (MeterSlots).

Needs — the wall, the rain and the flare

The wall is the theme, and the engine cannot draw it yet. A deck picture is stretched to cover the page, which turns a 128 px tile into a blur; this needs one that repeats at its own size, with the same grime on the modules. Then rain over it. The lights need a white-hot core down the middle of a lit tube and a lens flare, and their glow needs to run down the wall. The names need to glow, because they are the neon signs. Everything else on the page is a setting that exists today.

What it costs

This is a busy wall on purpose. Its brightest pixel is 1.9 : 1 against the wall around it, so it always sits behind the controls, but a dense page on a textured wall is slower to scan than one on a plain one. It is for a dark room, and the picker should say so. A flare never carries a state: a lit strip goes from a fifth of its color to all of it, 3.9 : 1 or better on every slot, so somebody who cannot see a flare still sees the light come on. The rain stands still when Windows is set to show fewer animations, and nothing flickers. The closest two colors are the cyan and the white neon, delta E 38.4 apart.

Seven asks, smallest first. Each is independent, and the page above uses all seven.

AskWhy Off-world Colonies needs itWithout it
1 Neon letters. A section's name, or a label that asks for it, glows in its own color around a core a third of the way to white. The section names and the page's name are the neon signs on the wall. A section can already say its name in its color (SectionNameInHue); this adds the light. Names in flat color.
2 A white-hot core. The lit part of a knob's arc, a fader's fill and a switch's strip gets a thinner, lighter line down its middle. That line is the difference between neon and paint. The strip is 2.5 px today, and its core needs 3. Color with a glow, which is Airy System.
3 The glow runs down. The light round a lit control reaches further below it than above: one offset on the glow that is there today. It is what makes the wall read as wet. Measured with print under it, a pad's glow has to be down to .12 where it reaches a name. A dry wall.
4 A lens flare. A lit light throws a streak along its length and a faint four-point star, in a flare color (alpha 0 means the light's own). The XY puck, the biggest light, also throws a ring. Only while it is lit. Every light in the film flares, and it is the first thing anyone remembers about how it looks. It has to be down to .12 where it reaches print, so the streak runs only a little past its own control. Lights that glow and do not flare.
5 Rain. One tiled layer of fine slanted streaks, under the controls, with a strength and a color. It can fall at a steady speed, and stands still when Windows asks for fewer animations. It is always raining. Drawn at .35, because that is the strongest it can be with dim print on top of it. A dry city.
6 A deck picture that repeats. Tiled at its own size instead of stretched to cover the page, with the theme's tile shipped in the app. The stains can be drawn into the same tile: 512 px is eight blocks across, too far apart for the eye to find the repeat. A section can lay the stains over itself too, dark only, so the modules are as dirty as the wall. The wall is the theme. The deck's picture today is stretched to the page, which is right for a photograph and turns a pattern into a blur. Dark only on a module, because a pale streak under print would take away from it; a dark one only adds. A plain wall. The theme does not work without this one.
7 Light in the room (can be dropped). A pool of light from above and soft bands of light through blinds, on the wall only. It is the light in every room in the film. On this page, the blinds fall on the bare wall at the right. The deck's gradient alone. Nothing else changes.

What I left out on purpose. Flicker: a neon sign that stutters is the most recognizable light in the film, and a flashing light on a performance surface is both a distraction and a hazard for people with photosensitive epilepsy. Screws and bolts on the modules: they would sell the idea, and the engine has nothing to draw them with that would not follow every section into every other theme. The giant moving billboards, the searchlights sweeping past, and any kind of lettering the film used. Where I was honest rather than faithful: the blocks are a motif drawn for this comp in the spirit of the textile-block walls in the film, not a copy of any real block. The colors were picked by eye from your stills and then every one was measured; the stills themselves are not in the repository.
