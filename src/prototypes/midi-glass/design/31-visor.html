Screen 31 — Visor, first proposed as Telemetry. Why the new name: to a lot of Windows users, "telemetry" means data collection, and a theme should not remind anyone of that. A visor is the glass in a pilot's helmet that a head-up display is drawn on, which is exactly this look. Vector, Wireframe and Hairline would fit as well. The look: thin light on a dark field. Every control is four corners, not a box, and only a control that is on has its corners closed into a frame. Knobs are rings of fine marks with a chevron riding the value, faders are rails pinched by two chevrons, and the XY field has a reticle. Nothing is filled until it is on.

Why the corners are 60 percent at rest. A resting corner measures 3.42 to 6.75 : 1 on the deck and 3.27 at its lit middle: enough to find every control, and quiet enough that forty of them do not turn the page into a grid of boxes. A lit line is 7.86 to 18.16. On closes the frame and lays a wash inside it, and the name keeps the print color, 11.34 to 13.66 : 1. So on never depends on telling one color from another: it is a closed frame among open corners.

Built — what the engine already does for this display

A dark deck lit in the middle (deck gradient), a fine dot grid laid over it as a repeating picture (imageRepeats), and a fall-off to the corners (vignettePercent). No plate and no shadow (glassTintPercent 0, plateElevation 0). Thin lines: arcs two pixels wide (arcThickness) that glow (arcGlow), rims one pixel (rimThickness). Marks round every knob (knobTickCount 37) and a scale beside every fader (faderScalePercent). A wash when on (fillWhenOnPercent 16) with the name kept in the print color (onInkColor). Sections as captions in their own color with no fill (sectionNameInHue, panelFill none). Meter zones in green, amber and red (meterSlots), and ice as the sixth color.

Needs — corners, longer marks, chevrons

A rim goes all the way round. Here a control at rest is only its corners, and on closes them into a frame. Every printed mark is the same length, so a scale reads as a comb; here every fourth mark on a knob and every fifth beside a fader is longer. A pointer is a line, a cap is a bar and a puck is a disc, and all three cover the scale; here they are chevrons and a reticle that point at it instead.

What it costs

Thin lines want a close screen or a big one. At arm's length on a small tablet, corners one and a half pixels wide are hard to find. Nothing is filled at rest, so the corners alone say where a control is. That is quick once you know the page, and slower for somebody new to it. It glows, so it is made for a dark room. The colors stay apart for color-blind players: the closest pair is cyan and violet, delta E 19.6 for a green-blind (deutan) player, and cyan and ice, 24 for a red-blind (protan) one. Print reads at 16.82 : 1 and dim print at 7.91.

Three asks, largest first. Each is independent, and the page above uses all three.

AskWhy Visor needs itWithout it
1 Corner brackets. A control's edge drawn as four corners rather than a full outline, in its color at a strength the theme names (here 60 percent). On, the corners close into a full frame with the wash inside. Held, the corners grow and glow. Sections get larger corners, and a knob under a finger gets corners too. The corners are the look. They also keep a busy page quiet: forty full outlines are a grid of boxes, and forty sets of corners are a field of targets in which the few that are on stand out, because only they are closed. A thin full outline at rest and a brighter one when on. It reads as a thin dark theme rather than a visor.
2 Major marks. The ring of marks round a knob and the scale beside a fader get a longer mark at an interval the theme names: here every fourth mark on a knob, every fifth beside a fader. A scale with every mark the same length is a comb. Longer marks let you read a position at a glance, which is the whole point of drawing a scale. Every mark the same length. The engine already draws both scales.
3 Chevrons and a reticle. A knob's pointer drawn as a small chevron riding its arc and pointing at the marks; a fader's cap as two chevrons pinching the rail at the value, with a hairline between them; the XY puck as a reticle, a ring with four ticks and an open middle. These are the shapes a head-up display uses to point at a number. A solid cap or puck would cover the very scale this look is built around. The engine's pointer line, cap bar and round puck, drawn thin.

What I left out on purpose. Horizon ladders, compass strips, altitude tapes and blinking warnings. They are the decoration of a real display, and here they would be numbers that mean nothing, on a surface where every number should mean something. The two readouts at the top are real: the tempo and the song position. Honest rather than faithful: a real visor is one color, usually green. This one keeps the six colors, because on a control surface the color is how you tell the synth from the drums. The type is Segoe UI Variable, spaced wide, and Cascadia Mono for numbers; that is the layout's choice, and the theme sets no typeface. Every color was measured.
