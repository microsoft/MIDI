Screen 29 — Chicago, the code name of Windows 95. A teal desktop, gray windows with title bars in color, and two pixel bevels on everything: raised is something you can press, pressed is something that is down, and sunken is a white field that holds a value. On is said by the bevel, not by color: a latched switch stays pressed and fills with a fine checkerboard, and a pad fills with its color only while its note sounds. Every value sits in a white field, because on the gray face half of this palette is too faint to read a value by.

Built — what the engine already does for this desktop

A flat teal deck with white ink on it and black ink in the windows (sectionInkColor). Windows filled with the gray face (panelFill color) and title bars in their color with the ink picked for each (sectionHeader filledBar). Square corners, no glow, no shadow (cornerRadius 0, glowStrength 0, plateElevation 0). White fields for the XY field and readouts (wellColor, wellFillsControl). A black pointer on the knob's cap (pointerOnCap, pointerColor), a printed scale beside every fader (faderScalePercent), meter zones in green, olive and maroon (meterSlots). Pads that fill with their color and switches that do not (padFillWhenOnPercent 100, fillWhenOnPercent 0). One pixel of travel (pressTravelPixels). The etched line: two one pixel stripes, gray over white (stripeColors, stripeWidth 1).

Needs — bevels, a pointed cap, a checkerboard, a tag

There is no two color edge today. A rim is one color, so a raised button and a sunken field look the same. Here the bevel is the whole language, and it has to land on whole screen pixels to look right. A fader cap is a bar; here it points at its scale. On is a fill or a lamp; here a latched switch is pressed in and checkered, and the playing step has a dotted line round it. A gray button has nowhere to show its color, so it gets a small tag before its name.

What it costs

It is gray. Most of the page is one mid gray, and the color lives in small places: title bars, tags and values. That is calm, and it is not a theme for a dark stage. The teal is loud, and a page of small windows shows a lot of it. Every edge is drawn twice, so a dense page is a busy one. The palette is small and dark: on the gray face, green, olive and teal measure 2.82, 2.31 and 2.62 : 1, which is why every value sits in a white field, where all six clear 4.20. Green and olive are the closest pair: delta E 11.8 for a green-blind (deutan) player and 4.1 for a red-blind (protan) one, so never use those two for opposite jobs. Black on the gray reads at 11.54 : 1.

Four asks. The first is the theme; the other three are small, and each is independent.

AskWhy Chicago needs itWithout it
1 Bevels. A two pixel edge in four named grays, drawn three ways: raised for a switch at rest, a window's frame and a knob's cap; pressed for a switch that is held or latched; sunken for anything that holds a value — a fader's slot, a knob's ring, a meter, a readout, the XY field, a lamp. It lands on whole screen pixels at every zoom, so it stays two crisp lines rather than a blur. The bevel is how this whole look says what a thing is. Raised means press me, pressed means I am down, sunken means read me. Without it, a button and a text box are the same gray rectangle. Flat gray boxes with a one color outline. It reads as a gray theme, not as Windows 95.
2 A pointed fader cap. The cap drawn as a five sided pointer aimed at the printed scale, with the raised bevel: pointing right on an upright fader, down on one that runs across. It is the one control shape everybody remembers from that era, and the point tells you which mark the fader is on. A plain raised bar for a cap.
3 Two pixel patterns. A switch that is latched on is filled with a one pixel checkerboard of white and the face, and the step that is playing gets a dotted line one pixel wide round it. Both are drawn in screen pixels, two by two at 200 percent, so they stay a fine texture. Pressed and checkered is how a latched toggle and the current taskbar button looked, and it says on without any color at all. The dotted line is the focus line, and it is the plainest way to say "this one, now". Latched is just pressed. The playing step gets the engine's usual outline.
4 A color tag before the name. A small square of the control's own color, with a one pixel dark edge, in front of the name on a switch, at rest and when on. The buttons are gray with black names, so the control's color needs somewhere to live, and a tag is what that era used to show a color. A gray button shows no color until it is on.

Why this one is safe. It is Microsoft's own look, from its own product. Even so, there is no logo, no Start button and no title bar buttons: a close box that does not close would be a lie on a surface where everything else does something. About the name: Chicago was also the classic Macintosh system font. Nothing here uses it, and if the name causes confusion, Ninety-Five would do as well. The type is Microsoft Sans Serif, which still ships with Windows; that is the layout's choice, and the theme sets no typeface. Where I was honest rather than faithful: the real thing had no knobs. These are a raised cap in a sunken white ring, which is how that era drew anything round you could turn. Every color was measured.
